\section{Literature Review}
\subsection{Traffic Sign Datasets and High-Resolution Panoramic Road Imagery}
Traffic sign detection datasets can be broadly categorized along two dimensions: how the data were collected and what the data describe. In terms of collection, datasets differ in camera sensors, which result in different resolutions (low-resolution crops versus high-resolution panoramic imagery), provenance (real-world captures versus synthetic data), and format (single images versus video sequences). In terms of content, datasets vary by geographic coverage (country-specific collections versus global collections), scene conditions (daytime versus nighttime lighting and weather such as sunny, foggy, or rainy), scene setting (urban versus rural environments), and class granularity (a few broad sign categories versus hundreds of fine-grained sign types). These factors strongly influence how well a model trained on a given dataset generalizes. Because traffic sign designs and road environments differ substantially across countries, we organize our review by geographic coverage and discuss three groups in turn: country-specific datasets from outside Taiwan, global multi-country datasets, and Taiwan-related datasets. Within each group, we compare datasets along the remaining dimensions, and we summarize all of them in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}.

\subsubsection{Country-specific datasets outside Taiwan}
Swedish Traffic Signs Dataset (STSD)~\cite{larsson2011using} is a real-world video-sequence dataset of Swedish roads, collected with a dashboard-mounted camera of highways and city streets. It contains over 20,000 frames, of which every fifth frame (20\%) is manually annotated, yielding 3,488 labeled traffic signs. Each sign is annotated with a bounding box and one of 7 class labels (pedestrian crossing, designated lane right, no standing or parking, priority road, give way, 50~kph, and 30~kph), together with its visibility status (visible, blurred, or occluded) and road status (the road being traveled or a side road). The data cover daytime summer conditions only.

LISA was introduced in~\cite{mogelmose2012vision} to make up for the lack of US traffic sign datasets, given the dominance of European ones. It contains 6,610 real-world images and video frames with 7,855 bounding-box annotations across 49 sign classes. Images were captured from in-vehicle cameras of various types, with resolutions from $640\times480$ to $1024\times522$ and sign sizes ranging from $6\times6$ to $167\times168$ pixels.

The German Traffic Sign Detection Benchmark (GTSDB)~\cite{houben2013detection} is a dataset of 900 real-world single images captured in Germany, covering urban, rural, and highway scenes under daytime and dusk conditions. It contains 1,206 manually annotated traffic signs under three broad categories (prohibitory, danger, and mandatory).

Belgian Traffic Signs Dataset (BelgiumTS)~\cite{timofte2014multi} is a real-world dataset of Belgian roads, collected with a van carrying eight roof-mounted cameras that captured $1628\times1236$ images mainly on small roads and streets. It contains 13,444 traffic sign annotations in 9,006 still images, corresponding to 4,565 physically distinct signs visible within 50~m of the camera. Each annotation is a bounding box, and the work focuses on 62 sign types, including all regular signs but excluding direction indicators with text.

Tsinghua-Tencent 100K (TT100K)~\cite{zhu2016traffic} is a large-scale real-world dataset of Chinese roads, built from 100,000 Tencent Street View panoramas captured across 10 regions in 5 cities, covering both urban and suburban scenes. Each panorama was cropped and sliced into $2048\times2048$ images, of which 10,000 contain 30,000 traffic signs annotated with bounding boxes, pixel-level masks, and class labels (warning, prohibitory, and mandatory), while the remaining images serve as negative samples. Signs are very small, often under 1\% of the image, and the data cover large variations in illuminance and weather, including occlusion, with only 45 classes having more than 100 instances.

CURE-TSD-Real~\cite{temel2019traffic} is a video-sequence dataset built on top of the BelgiumTS recordings of Belgian roads, with synthetically simulated challenging conditions added to real-world footage. In particular, each video was processed with 12 challenge types (decolorization, lens blur, Gaussian blur, codec error, darkening, exposure, dirty lens, noise, rain, shadow, snow, and haze) at five severity levels, using Adobe After Effects. This yields 896,700 images, including the challenge-free originals. Challenge type and level are provided as metadata, so the effect of a single factor on detection performance can be isolated, unlike in datasets where conditions vary simultaneously. Because the conditions are simulated rather than captured, the dataset covers Belgian roads only and does not capture natural variation in scene setting or geography.

DFG~\cite{tabernik2019deep} is a real-world dataset of Slovenian roads, collected by driving through several municipalities for traffic-sign inventory management. The dataset contains 6,957 single images, most at $1920\times1080$ and fewer than 200 at $720\times576$, with 13,239 traffic sign instances across 200 fine-grained classes, each with at least 20 instances. The data cover both rural and urban scenes, with natural occlusions, shadows, and conditions ranging from cloudy to direct sunlight. Because images were captured for inventory purposes, they were taken in daytime and avoid rain, snow, and fog, apart from a few images with slight snow cover.

\subsubsection{Global multi-country datasets}
Mapillary Traffic Sign Dataset (MTSD)~\cite{ertler2020mapillary} is, to our knowledge, the most geographically diverse traffic sign benchmark, built from 100,000 street-level images crowd-sourced through the Mapillary platform. Images were selected with a greedy per-country sampling scheme weighted by a global target distribution over continents (20\% North America, 20\% Europe, 20\% Asia, 15\% South America, 15\% Oceania, 10\% Africa), binned geographically to balance urban and rural areas, and filtered by predicted scene type (city street, highway, residential), camera type, and distance between samples to avoid near-duplicates. The images come from over 200 camera manufacturers and range from about 1 to over 16 megapixels, including 1,138 360-degree panoramas, and cover varied lighting and weather conditions. The dataset has two parts: 52,453 fully annotated images and 47,547 partially annotated neighboring images whose labels were generated automatically, intended for semi-supervised learning. The fully annotated set contains 257,543 bounding boxes covering all sign types, of which 82,724 are assigned to one of 313 fine-grained classes.

\subsubsection{Taiwan-related datasets}
Lu et al.~\cite{lu2022hierarchical} introduced a real-world dataset of Taiwanese traffic signs, built because existing foreign datasets lack Taiwan-specific sign designs and no Taiwanese dataset had been released for download. Images were captured with mobile phone cameras and a GoPro camera mounted inside a car; the phone photos were taken from 10 different angles and distances. It contains 11,074 images spanning 23 sign classes: 8 circular signs (6 prohibitory and 2 mandatory), 14 triangular warning signs, and one sign in an ``other'' category, so the classes also group into three shapes.

Li and Su~\cite{li2022traffic} collected their own real-world Taiwanese traffic sign dataset to validate their enhanced YOLOv5s6 model, since sign designs differ across regions. The dataset contains 2,364 images spanning 11 classes: Yellow Light, Two-stage Left Turn, Road Sign, Red Light, Watch Out for Children, Green Light, Keep Right, No Left Turn, Speed Measuring Enforcement, Maximum Speed 50, and No Parking.

Taiwan Traffic Sign (TWTS) dataset~\cite{suwattanapunkul2023efficient} is a real-world collection of Taiwanese roads, built because no public Taiwanese traffic sign dataset was available. Images were captured on roads in Taiwan under daytime, nighttime, and rainy conditions. It contains 837 images with 866 manually annotated traffic signs, labeled across 10 classes: keep right, hook left turn for motorcycles, no entry, pass on either side, and speed limits of 30, 40, 50, 60, 80, and 100~km/h.

TWTSDB~\cite{lin2026csformer} is a real-world dataset of Taiwanese roads, introduced to test generalization under domain shift, since sign designs and spatial distributions in driving scenes differ across locations. Images were captured with a windshield-mounted dashcam at $1920\times1080$ resolution, covering four metropolitan areas and highway, urban, and suburban road types. It contains 6,992 single images spanning 98 traffic sign categories.

In summary, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}, most existing datasets target a single country. This approach is reasonable since sign designs differ across countries, so a detector trained on one country's data cannot be applied directly to another. The issue is milder in Europe, where many signs are standardized by the Vienna Convention on Road Signs and Signals~\cite{mogelmose2012vision}, but it still suggests that each country needs its own dataset. Taiwan lacks one: several works report that no public benchmark exists for Taiwanese traffic sign detection~\cite{dewi2022deep,lu2022hierarchical,suwattanapunkul2023efficient,chang2024effective}. As a result, existing Taiwanese methods rely on self-collected datasets that are small, private, and time-consuming to build, which makes fair comparison between methods difficult~\cite{lu2022hierarchical,li2022traffic,suwattanapunkul2023efficient,lin2026csformer}. To address this gap, we introduce TW11K, a large-scale benchmark of Taiwanese traffic signs built from 11,878 high-resolution $8192\times4096$ panoramic images captured by a mobile mapping system. It contains 149,648 manually annotated instances across 21 classes. Because TW11K targets Taiwanese road infrastructure inspection and management, images were collected in daytime with clear visibility, following the approach of~\cite{tabernik2019deep}. More details are given in~\ref{sec:method}.

\subsection{Dataset Development, Annotation, and Characterization}
\subsubsection{Data collection}
We collected data with a mobile mapping system, a vehicle-mounted platform with three key subsystems: a laser scanner for point clouds, a camera for RGB images, and a positioning and orientation system that provides navigation, timing, and trajectory information. Since TW11K targets Taiwanese road infrastructure inspection and management, all images were captured in daytime under clear visibility conditions by a Ladybug5 panoramic camera. It has six fisheye lenses: five pointing sideways and one pointing up. The six images are stitched into a 360° view covering the full horizontal field of view, then projected into a 2:1 panorama through equirectangular projection. Unlike~\cite{zhu2016traffic}, who cropped the top 25\% and bottom 25\% of each panorama on the assumption that objects rarely appear there, we keep all panoramas intact. This lets users choose their own preprocessing. \hl{which city? urban or rural? capture intervals?}

\subsubsection{Data annotation}
Each object was manually annotated with a bounding box and a class label. In total, our 11,878 images contained 149,648 annotated objects across 21 classes. Following the national sign standard, we grouped Taiwanese traffic signs by function into ten groups: warning, mandatory, restriction, limit, guidance, assistance, barricade, construction, signal, and danger. Within each group, signs were further divided by shape (triangle, circle, rectangle, diamond, or arrow). Each class name therefore combines function and shape, and sign types sharing both are merged into a single class.This yields 21 final classes: warning triangle, warning rectangle, warning others, mandatory triangle, mandatory circle, mandatory rectangle, mandatory others, restriction circle, restriction rectangle, restriction others, limit circle, limit others, guidance rectangle, guidance circle, guidance arrow, guidance others, assistance rectangle, barricade rectangle, construction diamond, signal, and danger.

\subsubsection{Data statistics}
Fig.~\ref{fig:instances-per-class} shows that the annotated instances are highly imbalanced across classes. Signal and guidance rectangle dominate, with 49,231 and 39,316 instances, respectively, while the next most frequent class has only 12,861. At the tail, mandatory triangle, guidance circle, guidance arrow, and barricade rectangle have no instances in TW11K; they are retained in the taxonomy for future data expansion. Fig.~\ref{fig:instances-per-size} shows the number of instances per object size, following the definition of~\cite{lin2014microsoft}. In particular, objects with an area smaller than $32^2$ pixels are considered small, objects with an area between $32^2$ and $96^2$ pixels are considered medium, and objects with an area larger than $96^2$ pixels are considered large. In addition, we introduce a tiny category for objects with an area smaller than $16^2$ pixels, which is shown as a separate bar in the figure. The distribution is skewed toward small objects: there are 39,771 tiny and 61,418 small instances, compared with 41,410 medium and 7,049 large ones. Since tiny instances are a subset of small ones, they together account for the majority of annotations. Nevertheless, medium and large instances are far from negligible, which means that a detector tailored solely to small objects would struggle on a considerable portion of the data. A strong model must therefore handle a wide range of object scales.

Beyond the dominance of tiny and small instances, TW11K is made harder by its fixed panorama resolution of $8192\times4096$. At this scale, generic detectors suffer from feature disappearance~\cite{alilou2026small}: repeated downsampling layers progressively erase the few pixels that small objects occupy, making them nearly undetectable. Moreover, the substantial share of medium and large objects can dominate the gradient signal and destabilize the learning of small objects, which make up the majority of annotations. Finally, the stitching process and equirectangular projection introduce geometric distortion. Residual barrel distortion from each of the six camera views compresses and curves objects toward the periphery of each view, altering their apparent shape. Likewise, equirectangular projection stretches objects horizontally as their latitude moves away from the equator. An example of geometric distortion in TW11K is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:distortion}. Together, severe class imbalance, small-object dominance, ultra-high resolution, and projection-induced distortion make TW11K a challenging and valuable benchmark for traffic sign detection.
